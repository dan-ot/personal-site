# Resume

For those of you landing on this page, I assume you're a potential employer or team member. As such, I also assume you have the short/standalone version of this information already - via LinkedIn, a PDF, or an application process. I'll provide more detail on each item than I normally would on that assumption - if the short version was good enough, you wouldn't have looked at this one.

## Early Experience

I grew up in a work culture. My Dad had a family policy - even though we moved a lot (every couple of years on average), we always worked the property we lived on and left it better than it was when we arrived. There may have been a rental house or two we didn't do this for, given that can be bad form as a renter.

My first paid work was at 16 (that puts it sometime around 1997) - assistant for a Master Mason. I hauled facade rocks and mixed concrete, and watched a true master at work. After that, I did restaurant work (fry cook, dishwasher, bus boy). We had a miscommunication about time off, and I was fired. Within a week, I had reached out to my homeschool community and landed another job - so when they wanted me back to help cover a wedding party, I was unavailable.

That next job was H&H Products, a chemical company. They processed raw chemicals into cleaning products, and eventually expanded into leasing and service of commercial dishwashers to help move the products. H&H mixed, bottled, lableled, and distributed these products. I started in the warehouse (clearing out old inventory), worked the filling line, and manned their retail counter (it was not busy). From there, I moved into the office - using CorelDRAW to make labels and doing IT repair work. That led to assisting with accounting (during busy periods) and working on business planning and projections (mostly in Excel - Excel is magic). While at H&H, I got exposure to federal purchasing as a vendor - we had good business with the Army at Fort Bragg and Fort Jackson.

After that I moved to Texas - the plan was to work an office job with my family, but it didn't settle out that way. I ended up working some food service (Schlotzky's) and retail (DSW). I attempted to pivot back to office work, as a temp. I mostly worked receptionist jobs in a variety of industries (marketing and IT support stand out). Finally, I landed a job demoing food in Costco.

## Military Experience

Seeing that things weren't moving in any particular direction, I shifted gears and joined the Navy. I picked IT, because I was already pretty knowledgeable, and I didn't want my daily work to be far off from something I knew so that I'd have time and energy to keep growing. As a homeschooler, I had to hold a higher standard - normally, there's a workbook you can complete, an exam you can attempt, and a physical examination to complete to be promoted during boot camp. I was told these were mandatory for me or I would be dismissed.

My Navy career officially started in 2005. I went through boot camp and A-School in Chicago that winter (Chicago in the dead of winter is very...special). I was very dedicated to military bearing - I enjoy knowing and following the rules, and I have a lot of respect for position and authority. Because of that, I was honor graduate for my boot camp class; and I received top marks in all my A-School classes as well. By the time I graduated and went to my first command, I had been promoted.

My first command was in Yokosuka, Japan. I was there for about two years. I really enjoyed my time (if you paid taxes in the US from 2005 to 2008ish, thanks). I was responsible for helping maintain the land side of communications for 7th Fleet. I got to tour a couple ships as part of troubleshooting, but most of my experience was in the NOC. I had a really great Chief (manager) - I really learned how to care for your team from him. I had some good training there, too - I picked up what would be CompTIA Security+, CompTIA Networking+, and CISSO, but even though we learned from civilians, from civilian books, the certifications were not official. I also promoted very quickly - that was more from the IT profession, since enlisted members would get to the ranks of Petty Officer (E4 through E6) and then transition out to the contracted civilian workforce. The civilians did a lot of the actual work, got paid a lot more, and didn't have many of the regulations to obey.

Navy rotation requires alternating sea/ship time with shore time, so my next posting was on minesweepers. I mostly picked them because there was a base in Texas, near my family, so I'd finally be able to be home on some weekends - a huge shift from being 17 hours away. Life shipboard is very rough - physically and emotionally demanding. I got invaluable experience seeing the effects of policy, I had some exposure to very bad management (learning what not to do), and I got a closer look at federal purchasing from the consumer side. This was one of the lowest points in my life - it really was my brothers in arms who carried me through, and I'm eternally grateful for them (minesweepers were male-only crews). I was unconcerned about rank or status in this period, so I didn't progress very far.

## Immedaite Post-Military

Once I had done my six years of active duty (and was on my two years of reserve to fill the eight-year contract), I returned home - late 2011. I was determined not to drift, but to pursue my actual passions - I had wanted to build games since I was 10. As part of that process, I met and married my wife, Christy. Christy is very business-minded, so I started backing her up with Wordpress customization - this engendered my personal dislike for PHP.

On the job front, I landed a position at *VersaSuite*, an electronic medical record company. I started in customer support - handling calls from small medical offices to deal with the fallout of errors or bugs. One of our main tools was reaching directly into SQL databases to repair the output of bugs after the software had been patched. I formed some strong opinions based on seeing programming output from that angle; also, the software was written in C++ and my experience did nothing to sell the language to me as effective.

From there I went to the custom tool department - we'd build a UX specific to a practice or specialty with direct feedback from the doctors who would use it. That part of the feedback loop was very interesting - I also got a lot of visibility into the back office of the medical industry, and an interesting view into how it looks to attempt to implement regulations as they emerge. I was put in charge of the group from my 'leadership' experience in the Navy. it was a very different environment, trying to match skillsets with tasks when I didn't have any levers over either the scope of the work or the people we hired (beyond my step in the interview process). I don't think I got it right...I may also have been a pushier or more awkward boss than I prefer, just from learning the skills as I went.

It was a good introduction to software as a business and a craft. We worked in a custom toolset with an in-house scripting language loosely based on Excel formulas; our API surface extended whenever one of the senior C++ developers had a little extra time, which was rare. I also got my first foray into higher-level languages - some of the main software engineers wanted to use C#, and some of my team wanted to use Python, so I got a little exposure to both. However, I still wasn't headed in quite the right direction, so I decided to lean on my GI Bill benefits for my next step.

## Eductaion

I completed an *Associate of Applied Sciences in Game Design* from *Austin Community College* from 2013 to 2015. In that same period, I got about halfway through their Game Art program as well (there was a lot of credit overlap).

This program was fantastic. All the instructors had industry experience - the rough, layoff, studio-out-of-business kind - but also the kind that applied to games I had played and loved. They brought the full breadth of that experience to their teaching. The program covered the whole cycle, from design docs to art, code, and project management - all the way to a playable prototype at the end. It was invaluable. I met some folks that I truly hope to work with again in a game capacity - even though I think many of them have left the industry with no desire to return.

## Recent Experience

In 2014 I followed a lead from someone my wife had previously worked with to land a position at *Nexersys*. My initial role was, once again, IT support - but that was mostly the way in the door. They were building exercise equipment with a heavy focus on gamification; their newest product was planned to use Unity, and that was the engine I had just been learning in school. Nexersys was a very small company - 10 employees, with only 3 on the engineering group. I worked with a firmware specialist (Master's in Electrical Engineering and a passion for machine learning), a generalist who could handle C++ (for an embedded Linux engine) or Objective-C (for the iOS port of the new product), and a generalist who had Unity experience. They were an amazing team, and I'm very proud of the work we did together - I'd work with any of them again in a heartbeat.

This was where I really understood Scrum and Agile as practical methods. We read the book and just followed it, without getting pulled off by a specific subculture or reinterpretation. The focus was on clarity, self-scrutiny, and continuous improvment of our skills and process. I also got to see the business side of it in action - the owner was a professional inventor, who had a few 'must-do' ideas a week, so I saw how Agile was truly useful in the context of a large number of uknowns and helping to balance constantly-shifting priorities. As a team, we also read over and discussed things like design patterns (Gang of Four and the newly-completed Game Design Patterns).

I also got experience in Java (working on the Android port - I had to write a Unity plugin that could connect to Bluetooth and intepret the custom firmware signals), decent exposure to C (for firmware) and C++, and a lot of experience in C# for Unity. I got a peek inside manufacturing logistics, including needed to interop with a factory in China. And, very fortunately, the owner was very willing to talk about small business and creative process, so I got a really wide exposure to the factors in which an engineering team operates. One of the last projects I worked on was a C# CLI to be integrated into the quality check process - a command they could run to introspect the firmware. That gave me a lot of confidence with C#/.Net outside of Unity as well.

In October of 2015, word came down (as it does in most startups) that we'd be missing payroll that month. It wasn't something I could support with also losing the GI Bill money after graduating college, so I reached out to several jobs I found online (Stack Overflow Jobs, if I remember correctly) to local game companies.

## Game Industry

The important response I got was from *PlayStudios*, a company with several slot game apps. I was hired in November of 2015 as a *Web Developer*. I wrote C# server code, maintained legacy ASP pages, and originated an SPA/API tool to handle direct marketing. I was part of the centralized Platform group that supported all of the various individual game teams across the company. I learned a lot, very quickly - it was a medium-sized company, but the pace and demands were still pretty rigorous. I was introduced to Domain-Driven Design and heavily influenced by it - I still consider it core curriculum for good engineering. I worked on a small team, and we still followed Agile pretty closely (and I like to think, effectively).

I got to see some areas that I hadn't seen in a while, and from a more involved perspective. I saw the difference between authoring SQL queries and actual DBA/DBE work; I got exposed to DevOps on a multi-team scale, including cross-platform builds; I observed the effort required in maintaining an on-prem Dev stack and a data-center-based remote Prod stack. I also started working with NoSQL/Document databases, and in-memory caches (memcache and later Redis). Additionally, I dealt with basic software lifecycle issues - the cost of greenfield vs technical debt, and balancing known legacy systems against using something newer.

After a few years building on the tools and platform side, I was able to move over to *Game Server Engineering*. Slots games have a core game loop that is heavy probabilities and math. Players generally transact with the system in 1-3 second increments (the length of the spin animation when you press the 'hurry up' button), and we had upwards of a million players. The system was very high-throughput, yet still atomically transactional within a single request-response cycle. It was an interesting set of limitations to work through.

My specific part was a 'Features' engineer - those parts of the game which weren't the core loop. We had to slide into the same tight request-response cycle, but our features would interact with the core game board and response - for example, a 'pick three' minigame, or a 'find 10 Queen symbols' event. We worked in a high-operations environment - configuration had to be dynamic (changeable multiple times a day without a release of client or server) and on a schedule. In working with the core Game Server engineers, I picked up functional programming - it worked really well with the high-performance demand, my user-facing and domain-driven sensibilities, and making rapid change in an enormously complex code environment.

From there I moved to *Lead Game Server Engineer* supporting a different one of the slot apps. I continued in the same vein; however, we added some complexity on top. The person in charge of Product had a very good sense of merchandising - the similar sense that puts fresh fruit at the entrance of a grocery store and the junk food near the registers, or puts kid-targeting products at kid-eye-height on the shelves. Under his direction we developed a sophisticated storefront and catalog engine, and some tooling to support it.

In additon to the increased technical complexity and more direct iteration with Product folks, I had direct reports. I was deeply involved in staffing for the server team, including working out head counts, reviewing resumes, and orchestrating interviews. I also worked on goal setting, one-on-ones, and skills improvement - in addition to technical reviews and feedback and specific feature planning and whiteboarding. I still delivered a feature or two, or made tool improvements, but the majority of my time was in meetings. Despite having spent so much time as an individual contributor, I really enjoyed this part of the job as well. We didn't have meetings just to have them, but we'd come away with a deliverable - a skill to learn, an administrative hurdle for me to clear for a team member, a whiteboard design to implement. I was very satisfied being support while others delivered actual code and operational features.

At this point, the company was looking at going public. We were also looking to scale our portfolio, through outsourcing, acquisition, and just plain hiring. Our particular game didn't have an official technical representative in cross-team meetings and conversations, so I was promoted to *Tech Director*. At one point I had 7 direct reports, one of whom was the Client Engineering manager with 10 reports of his own; we got an amazing Server Lead and I was down to 3 reports again. My daily work and consideration was much more long-range: questions of 'build or buy'; 'cloud or on-prem', 'hire, contract, or outsource'. I worked on org charts, career planning, and inter-office politics. One of my projects was to facilitate a deep, team-wide, all-levels discussion on our game's Mission, Vision, and Values; it was imperative to me that it not be a dry corporate exercise to produce a stack of buzzwords, but an actual expression of who we were as a team, and where we wanted to go. It produced a set of artifacts we could hearken back to in any given meeting to help narrow our decision-making to just those options which were actually in line with our team.

Tech Director was a very satisfying position; I got to apply my previous sensibilities for business with my ability to see the customer/user/player's side of things, and cross all kinds of skill borders to bring people together. I enjoyed understanding, curating, and interacting with the team culture as a whole, and each of the individuals that made it. However, once the company went public, the upper culture changed; the focus was on outsourcing and cheapness of labor, not quality of results or care for people. I wasn't in any budget conversations or decisions, which made it very difficult to make a meaningful decision. And we brought on a project manager (Producer, in game industry lingo) who felt that managers were an artifact of factory capitalism and were entirely superfluous.

So, my final role at PlayStudios was *Software Architect*. I was still involved in the high-level conversations about what we would do next as an engineering culture, and I was still able to help guide the learning and careers of my team-mates, which I appreciated. However, I wasn't happy with the ways the culture had changed - down to the necessity to conceal financials for other teams because of being publicly traded. I felt things were going off-course again, so I put out my feelers and started applying around.

## Current Day

In January of 2022, I landed my current job - *Senior Solutions Architect*. At the time it was with Activision-*Blizzard*-King; this was pre-merger, it's now just Blizzard (as part of XBox Studios). It was a recurring theme in reivews throughout my career that I didn't speak up enough, so I wanted to take a role to challenge that a bit. My position is to understand the shared Battle.net platform, and help game teams navigate their options to produce the features they want. It's somewhere between cusomter support for game team engineers and my work as a software architect. It's a critical role, and a real platform differentiator for Battle.net; I have an excellent manager and team and I'm glad to be contributing.
